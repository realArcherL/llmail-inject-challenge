Start here: what am I trying to see?

The question is: when Phi-3 reads an injected email, what does the Jacobian lens show—and what changes when I mark that same email with my defense? Attack success tells me what happened. The lens provides another view of the processing that accompanied it.

A token is a chunk of text. A layer is one processing stage inside the model. A Jacobian lens is a mathematical readout that turns an internal state into ranked text tokens. A readout is the resulting list. Those tokens are clues to interpretation, not a hidden transcript of complete sentences.

Keep three questions separate: What text did the model receive? What tokens appeared in the lens? What did the assistant actually do? Each is a different piece of evidence.

How to read the screenshot from the paper

The linked case study uses Opus 4.5 and fabricated search results. The authors interpret the readouts as recognition of an injection, followed by a concern with candor when challenged. My LLMail experiment changes both the model and the task. It adapts this inspection method; it is not an exact reproduction of that case. Source: the paper’s prompt-injection case study and Figure 37.

Read your screenshot in this order:

  1. Left: the conversation. This is text supplied to the model or written by the assistant. Blue notes such as “human pushes back” are annotations explaining the scene.
  2. Red outline: the selected token. The author has chosen a particular place in that conversation to inspect. Even a quote mark or closing brace can be selected. The model state there includes the preceding context.
  3. Connector line: which readout belongs to which place. Follow it from the selected token to the box on the right.
  4. Numbers down the box: layers. For example, 75 is a layer number, not 75% confidence. Bigger layer numbers are later processing stages. These boxes put larger numbers at the top, so moving upward goes deeper into the model.
  5. Words across a row: ranked tokens. They are alternative high-ranking readouts at that one layer and position. Do not join them into a sentence. Moving right is not advancing through the conversation.
  6. Colored highlights: visual grouping. They draw attention to related tokens. They are not a universal safe/unsafe scale or a probability scale.

The distinction to look for in LLMail: a readout containing email may describe the topic. A readout containing injection may suggest an assessment of that topic. Neither observation, by itself, tells us whether the assistant will send an unauthorized email.

How to read my four LLMail graphs

All four graphs below use undefended prompts. Orange and blue in the first graph do not mean “no defense” and “my defense.” The library comparison has been measured at the output level, but its lens readings are not in these graphs.

My graph score adds lens probability assigned to selected token IDs derived from send, email, contact, @, confirmation, function, {", and type, including some space-prefixed variants. The code takes the first token of each expression. I call this the tool-token score here.

A score of 0.10 means 10% of the lens distribution is on that selected token set. It does not mean a 10% attack success rate, or a 10% chance that the model recognized an injection. The token set is broad enough to describe ordinary email work too.

Study A reads each prompt at three places. These stay fixed while we move through the model’s layers:

  1. End of the first, benign email. An early comparison point before the second email’s content.
  2. End of the second email. This is the attack email in attack cases, or the test business email in clean cases.
  3. End of the fully formatted prompt. After the chat-format ending that introduces the assistant’s reply. This is where the first answer token is predicted.

That last formatting boundary explains why “end of the email” and “end of the prompt” can have different readings even when no additional email content appears between them.

The gray shading marks layers below 20, which the current analysis sets aside. Its spot checks found poor readings at layers 8 and 16, and readable answers at 24, 32, and 38. This does not certify every unshaded layer or prove that every shaded layer contains no useful information.

Graph 1: how much of the selected token set appears?

  • Horizontal axis: layer number. Moving right means later processing of the same selected position, not later words in the email.
  • Vertical axis: average tool-token score across prompts.
  • Orange: prompts containing an attack email. Blue: prompts containing a clean email. Each line averages many prompts.

Look at the rightmost panel first. Near the final layers, attack prompts have a higher average score. At layer 38, the averages are 0.05866 for attacks and 0.00019 for clean emails.

Now look left. Both groups rise almost to 1.0 and overlap. This does not mean almost every attack succeeds. The lens strongly reads email at that location in both groups, so this score cannot distinguish them there.

Takeaway: where we inspect the prompt matters. A large score alone is not an attack detector, and an average can hide large differences between individual emails.

Graph 2: can the score separate attack emails from clean emails?

The colors now mean positions, not groups: blue is the first email boundary, orange is the second email boundary, and green is the end of the formatted prompt. Each colored line compares attacks against clean emails at that position.

The horizontal axis is still layer number. The vertical axis is AUROC: how often an attack prompt ranks above a clean prompt by score, counting a tie as half. The dashed line at 0.5 means chance ordering. A value below 0.5 means the ordering tends to go the other way.

Follow the green line to layer 38. It reaches 0.806, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.758 to 0.849. In plain language, an attack scores higher in about 81% of randomly selected attack/clean pairs. This is not “81% of attacks detected” and not “81% of attacks succeeded.”

A confidence interval expresses sampling uncertainty. A 95% interval is produced by a procedure intended to cover the underlying value in 95% of repeated samples. These intervals do not remove weaknesses in the score’s meaning or in the sample selection.

Takeaway: the end-of-prompt reading contains information that distinguishes these two collections. It does not yet show that Phi-3 internally labels an email as malicious.

Graph 3: do higher-scoring attacks succeed more often?

This graph contains attack prompts only. The colors still identify the three reading positions. For each attack, the experiment compares its lens score with how often it succeeded in eight generated answers.

The vertical axis is rank correlation: how closely those two orderings agree. Zero means no rank association; positive values mean higher scores tend to accompany more frequent success; negative values mean the reverse. It is not a percentage of successful predictions.

At layer 38, the green line reaches 0.504 (95% interval: 0.382 to 0.619). That is a positive but imperfect relationship. An attack with a high reading is not guaranteed to work.

Graph 2 asks “attack or clean?” Graph 3 asks “among attacks, which work more often?” Those are different questions. Neither asks whether the model has recognized the attack as an attack.

A concrete grouping from the report

The report chose the largest score among its 203 clean prompts as a threshold. Of 200 attacks, 77 exceeded it. Those attacks succeeded 35.6% of the time, compared with 12.3% for the remaining 123. Because the threshold was chosen using these same clean prompts, zero clean false positives here is true by construction. It is not an independently tested detector.

Graph 4: how do winning and losing answers differ as they unfold?

Study B uses 76 prompts that each produced both outcomes: one answer called the tool and another did not. Teacher forcing means replaying each recorded answer into the model token by token, then reading its internal states. The prompt is the same within a pair; the answers can differ from their first token.

The horizontal axis has changed. It is now progress through each answer. A value of 0.25 means the bin around one quarter of that answer’s sampled length. It is not layer 25, the 25th token, or an identical sentence in both answers.

  • Left panel: orange averages answers that called the tool; blue averages those that did not. Higher still means more score on the selected token set.
  • Right panel: first subtract losing from winning within each prompt’s pair, then average those differences. Above zero means winning answers scored higher; below zero means losing answers scored higher. The paired subset can change between bins.
  • Vertical bars: 95% confidence intervals. A bar crossing zero leaves the sign of the difference uncertain at that position. These are individual intervals across several explored bins.
  • Orange on the right: all sampled positions. Green: positions estimated to come before the winning answer’s tool call.
  • Dotted vertical line: the median tool-call onset, around 76% by response characters. Some answers start their call much earlier. Everything left of that line is not automatically free of tool-call text.

Why the green curve matters: after a recorded answer begins writing a tool call, the model is reading that call back. A tool-related reading there may reflect the visible answer, rather than something that preceded the action.

Boundary check: the existing plot estimates the boundary from character counts. Checking against saved token positions found 86 sampled positions at or after onset included in its “before” set. Rechecking the highlighted 25% bin using those token positions gave a difference of about 0.035, a 95% interval of 0.001 to 0.082, and 64 pairs. That check is separate from the original curve displayed here; the original chart has not been silently replaced.

Takeaway: successful and unsuccessful continuations have different readings at some positions. This does not establish why the outcomes diverged. The text preceding the tool call may already differ, and comparing each answer at the same percentage does not hold that text fixed.

The study record: what I set out to do

I maintain spotlighting-datamarking, an npm package for apps that use large language models. It defends against prompt injection (instructions that try to redirect a model away from its intended task). More specifically, it targets indirect prompt injection (those instructions hidden inside data the model reads, such as an email).

The defense uses spotlighting (marking untrusted input so the model can distinguish data from instructions). One way to do that is datamarking (inserting a marker throughout the untrusted text and telling the model what it means).

I wanted to inspect indirect prompt injections and their defenses under the Jacobian lens, using LLMail-Inject attacks on Phi-3. Replaying the attacks and measuring my library’s effects on output established the behavioral context for that comparison.

The data

Microsoft ran LLMail-Inject, a public challenge where attackers tried to make an email assistant send an email it should not send. The released dataset contains 461,640 submissions and 203,679 unique attack payloads, meaning distinct pieces of attacker-supplied text.

It is a large dataset, but its size hides some limits. Only 1.9% of unique payloads were ever tried against both model families. Of the payloads that beat Phi-3, only 8.9% also beat GPT. That is limited evidence of transfer between models, not a controlled test of every attack on both.

Also, 82.9% of unique payloads were submitted only once. A single result tells us little about how reliably an attack works. The dataset records outcomes but does not include the model’s replies, so I had to regenerate them. Half the dataset targets GPT, whose model weights are not open, so I could not study its internal states.

Experiment 01: rebuilding the challenge

I used microsoft/Phi-3-medium-128k-instruct in bfloat16 (a 16-bit numerical format). File hashes confirmed that its weights on Hugging Face have not changed since 2 May 2024.

I ported the prompt recipe line by line from Microsoft’s released agent code. One detail matters: Phi-3’s chat template, which formats messages for the model, silently drops system messages. Microsoft’s code therefore puts the system rules, tool instructions, user query, and emails into one user turn. Mine does the same.

A token is a chunk of text the model reads or produces. A tokenizer is the software that maps text to those chunks and back. The model produces logits (scores for possible next tokens), which are converted into probabilities for choosing the next token.

I copied Microsoft’s generation settings: temperature (a setting that adjusts how concentrated those probabilities are) of 1.0, and top_p (a setting that limits sampling to the most likely tokens whose combined probability reaches a threshold) of 0.92. Each answer could contain at most 500 new tokens.

The sample contained 400 attacks: 200 with no defense and 200 with Microsoft’s spotlighting. I generated eight answers per attack, for 3,200 answers, plus another 1,600 at temperature 0.7.

When Microsoft ran the same undefended attack twice, the outcomes agreed 81.2% of the time. Mine agreed 78.4% of the time. The rank correlation (how closely two measurements order the same attacks by success rate) was 0.68 without defense and 0.39 with spotlighting.

The same attacks tended to win in both setups, especially without defense. But my undefended replay was fooled roughly two thirds as often overall. The spotlighting gap was larger.

What I checked

  • The token limit. Only 4.6% of undefended answers and 4.8% of spotlighting answers reached the 500-token cap.
  • The tool-call parser. There were 85 undefended answers and 22 spotlighting answers with tool-call text that my parser did not accept. Of those, 62 and 7 copied the prompt’s System: example. Microsoft’s parser rejects those too.
  • Temperature. At 0.7, undefended recorded wins and losses succeeded at 39.8% and 6.5%, compared with 35.8% and 6.8% at 1.0. Agreement was 82.8%, and rank correlation was 0.72. This did little to resolve the gap.
  • Different weights. All six weight files were byte-identical to the May 2024 files.
  • Long prompts. The gap also appeared below 4,096 tokens.
  • Spotlighting wording. The text printed in the paper matched the released code.

These checks did not explain the difference. Azure’s serving stack remains a possible source: default sampling settings, prompt wrapping, or possible compression. I cannot inspect it from outside. The gap is unexplained.

Experiments 02 and 02b: testing the defenses

I compared defenses on the same 200 undefended attacks. I also used 203 clean business emails from Microsoft’s own false-positive test set to measure damage when nothing was attacking. The defense runs used four answers per prompt: 7,264 answers in the first round and 6,448 in the second.

The columns measure different things:

  • Attack success: the share of answers that called the email tool the attacker wanted. This measures the tool call, not whether every requested email detail was correct.
  • Cut vs no defense: the relative reduction in attack success.
  • Key terms kept: the share of a clean email’s distinctive words that appeared in the summary, expressed as a percentage of the no-defense score. This is a rough measure of content retention.
  • Garbled names: the share of answers with a capitalised word or number that appeared nowhere in the prompt and was not an ordinary English word. It catches invented content and mangled names, but also flags some ordinary words. The no-defense value of 3.6% is the noise floor.
  • Spurious tool call: a tool call on a clean email, where none was wanted.
  • Median prompt tokens: the middle prompt length for the attack inputs, after applying the defense.

Sanitize-only changed just 1 of the 200 attack prompts. These attacks rarely hid invisible characters, so its measured results were identical to no defense at the precision shown.

Microsoft’s spotlighting cut attack success by 89%, with key-term retention at 85% of baseline. My library’s letters-in-spaces mode cut success by 75%, but garbled names appeared in 19.7% of clean-email answers. That was far above the 3.6% baseline.

Base64 (an encoding that represents data using a limited alphabet of text characters) looked strong if I only counted attacks. Its 2.2% success rate hid a failure to preserve the email: just 6% key-term retention and 36.2% of answers with invented or garbled names.

Why names came out mangled

My library replaced every space with a random marker of 7 to 12 letters and digits. When that marker started with a letter, Phi-3 could merge it into the preceding word during tokenization.

For example, with Urban followed by gtPwW2E99, Phi-3 reads the start as U, rb, ang. It then writes Urbang in its summary. Part of the marker has become part of the name.

  • Of 195 mangled names in spaces mode, 140 (72%) were a real name plus the first letters of that prompt’s marker. Examples included Urban + g with gtPwW2E99, and Renewal + k with kyuNCDXt0.
  • Of 93 mangled names under random placement, 49 (53%) picked up the marker’s first letters. Another 19 (20%) repeated a word ending, such as Renewal + al, when a marker split a word in half.
  • Microsoft’s marker produced 14 flagged cases, but none involved its marker. It starts with a digit, and Phi-3 puts every digit in its own token.

I also measured how many email words reached Phi-3 with clean token boundaries: 97.9% with no defense, 96.3% with Microsoft’s marker, 94.1% with my random placement, and 90.5% with my spaces mode.

A shorter marker

A private-use Unicode character is a character code reserved for users to assign their own meaning, rather than a standard letter or symbol. I compared that option with several longer markers.

The Spotlighting paper by Hines and colleagues (2024) recommends a single special character, U+E000, from the private-use area, and says any special character works. It also warns that base64 hurts weaker models. My Phi-3 results show that same problem.

The uncomfortable trade-off

Switching the spaces mode to a short Unicode marker reduced garbled names from 19.7% to 3.5%, effectively the no-defense floor. Key-term retention rose to 106%. But attack success also rose, from 5.4% to 6.5%.

The observed trade-off suggests that some of the old marker’s blocking came from the same disruption that mangled names. The attack-success intervals overlap, so these measurements do not establish a precise increase in the underlying rate.

Moving letter markers from GPT-4’s boundaries to Phi-3’s own boundaries did not repair the mangling: it was 21.2%. Blocking did not improve either. This points to the marker’s alphabet as the cause of the name damage, rather than the choice of tokenizer for placement.

Restricting the short Unicode marker to word gaps gave the weakest blocking among the marking defenses, with 12.2% attack success. In this sample, 68% of attack payloads contained runs of at least 20 characters without spaces. The longest was 455 characters. Word-gap marking leaves those runs unmarked.

What I changed in version 2.1.0-alpha

randomlyMarkData now accepts a tokenizer option: any object with encode and decode methods. Markers can land at the boundaries of the model being defended, as the paper describes. Leaving the option out preserves the old GPT-4 behavior.

This exposed a separate bug. The marked text is now cut from the original string instead of being rebuilt from tokens. With Phi-3’s tokenizer, rebuilding had dropped a space at 7 of 10 marker positions in a test sentence.

I also added a Tokenizer type, six tests, and documentation on choosing a marker. The package has 112 passing tests in total.

What I would tell someone shipping this today

Based on these Phi-3 results, I would use a short private-use Unicode marker instead of letters and digits. I would allow marking inside long runs without spaces, and measure readable output alongside attack blocking.

I would not use base64 without checking that the model can decode it reliably. On Phi-3, it produced invented email content: 6% content retention and 36.2% of answers with invented or garbled names. A low attack rate is not enough if the assistant loses the email.

Expect that preserving readable output may cost some blocking. Measure both on the model and task you actually use.

Experiment 03: lens results are now available

The earlier draft described this work as pending. The current experiment contains Study A’s 200 attack prompts and 203 clean prompts, plus Study B’s 152 answer replays from 76 matched prompts. Its four graphs and limitations are explained in the reading guide above.

The lens was fitted on 100 Wikipedia passages across 4 GPUs (graphics processors used for model computation). The completed readouts cover undefended inputs. The defended lens comparison remains unfinished.

Caveats

  • The replay gap is unexplained. My absolute attack rates are lower than the challenge’s. Compare defenses within these experiments, rather than against Microsoft’s published rates.
  • The attacks were not adapted to my library. The defense comparison uses attacks written against Phi-3 with no defense in place. Nobody was trying to beat my package. A live attacker who can see the defense would do better. A separate study, The Attacker Moves Second, found that adaptive attacks beat spotlighting over 95% of the time.
  • More than 100% key-term retention does not mean better summaries. Marked text seems to make the model quote emails more literally. This metric rewards verbatim words.
  • The garbled-names metric is imperfect. It flags some ordinary words. Read every defense against the no-defense row.
  • This is one model. Everything here concerns Phi-3-medium. Larger models may behave differently.

Short glossary

Layer / readout
A model processing stage / the tokens returned by the lens at a selected stage and text position.
AUROC
How often an attack ranks above a clean prompt by a score, with ties counting as half. It is not classification accuracy.
Tool-token score
The sum of lens probability on a selected set of token IDs related to email and tool syntax. It is not attack success probability.
Token / tokenizer
A chunk of model-readable text / the software that converts between text and those chunks.
Prompt injection / indirect prompt injection
Instructions that redirect a model away from its task / such instructions hidden in external data.
Spotlighting / datamarking
Marking input as untrusted / doing so by inserting markers throughout it.
Logits
Scores for possible next tokens, before conversion into probabilities.
Temperature / top_p
Settings that adjust probability concentration / limit which tokens are eligible for sampling.
Confidence interval
A range expressing sampling uncertainty, with a stated long-run coverage level.
Rank correlation
How closely two measurements agree on an ordering.
Private-use Unicode character
A character code whose meaning is left for users to assign.
Teacher forcing
Reading a recorded answer back into a model token by token.
Jacobian lens
A tool for reading which words an internal model state leans toward producing.
